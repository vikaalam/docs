## In one paragraph

**`workspace-ops` is the single home for a company's operational layer.** When something happens in
a source system — a deposit lands, a trade settles, a filing is due — it reacts automatically, runs
any multi-step process, assigns the human work with a deadline, tells the right people, calls the
right vendors, produces the regulatory reports, and records every change permanently and auditably.

It **never owns the customer, the money, or the account**. It owns the *reaction*, the *process*, and
the *work* performed on them, referring to business entities only through an opaque reference. That
one constraint is what lets a single codebase deploy at any company.

## The dividing principle

<Note>
  **Source systems state facts. `workspace-ops` decides the reaction, runs the process, and assigns
  the work. Business entities never change hands.**
</Note>

Every rule in the design descends from that sentence. Its most important consequence is the second
half: this platform is never the system of record for a business entity. It does not own the
customer, the transaction, the balance, or the account.

That is not modesty — it is portability. A system that copies customer records is bound to one
company's customer schema forever. A system that only *references* them can be deployed anywhere.

| Change | What it touches |
| --- | --- |
| A new event needs to notify someone | **Zero deploys.** Configuration |
| A new step is added to the onboarding flow | **Zero deploys.** A new process definition version |
| The identity-verification vendor is replaced | **One connector** plus one binding row. No process definition changes |
| The notification vendor is replaced | **One driver file** |
| A whole new operational domain is added | **One new module**, plus one registry line. Existing modules untouched |

## Eight modules, one deployment

Each module has exactly one job, and each answers exactly one question.

| Module | What it does | Answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **directory** | People, teams, and who is responsible for what | "Who should do this, or be told?" |
| **automation** | Event ingress, workflow matching, an ordered list of actions | "This happened — what is the immediate reaction?" |
| **process** | Long-running, resumable, versioned multi-step cases | "Where is this case, and why is it stuck?" |
| **task** | A unit of human work: assignment, SLA, form, comments, attachments | "What do I have to do today?" |
| **notification** | Last mile to a person — recipient, channel, hand-off | "Tell the right people, on the right channel" |
| **integration** | Connectors to vendors and their callbacks. The only outbound door | "Call the vendor — without the caller knowing which vendor" |
| **reporting** | Report definitions, runs, artifacts, and proof of submission | "Produce the report and prove it was filed" |
| **audit** | Query and export of the immutable activity record | "Who changed what, when, and what did it look like before?" |

They are eight *code* boundaries, not eight menus. The interface groups screens by what a person came
to do — **Work · Signals · Configure · Records** — because an operator completing a compliance review
should never need to know their work was born in `automation` and materialised by `task`.

## Never a silent success

The single behavioural promise the whole design is measured against.

If an event finds no workflow, if a notification resolves to nobody, if a subject cannot be
recognised, or if an action dies after its retries — **it becomes a visible queue item for a person.**
Nothing is dropped quietly, and nothing that reached nobody is ever coloured green.
