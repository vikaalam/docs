description: "The questions asked in an assessment, and the honest answers"

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Can anyone delete or edit an audit entry?" icon="lock">
    No. The application's database role holds `INSERT` and `SELECT` on the audit table and **nothing
    else**. There is no code path that can update or delete an entry, because the privilege does not
    exist.

    A separate, more privileged credential exists only for the scheduled retention job that drops
    aged partitions — and that job is itself audited.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What if the audit write fails?" icon="triangle-exclamation">
    **The business change rolls back.**

    The entry is written inside the same transaction as the change it describes. An audit trail that
    is *sometimes* written is equivalent to none, so a failure to record is treated as a failure to
    act.

    It surfaces as a server error, never as a silent skip.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Is an attempt that was refused recorded?" icon="ban">
    Yes — and this took a specific design fix to make possible.

    A denied permission or a four-eyes violation means something threw. The exception propagates, the
    transaction rolls back, **and would take the audit entry with it**. The store built to prove a
    denial occurred could never retain one.

    So a `rejected` entry commits in **its own transaction**. That is safe because there is no
    business change for it to be atomic with.

    One consequence, in the right direction: **an over-recorded denial is a nuisance; an unrecorded
    one is a hole.**
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do we honour an erasure request without destroying evidence?" icon="eraser">
    By destroying the **key**, not the record.

    Personal data in long-retention stores is encrypted with a per-subject key. Erasure destroys that
    key. The record survives, stays countable, and remains part of the history; the personal data
    inside it becomes permanently unreadable.

    The destruction is itself recorded, which is what makes the erasure **provable** — the half of the
    obligation that a deletion cannot satisfy.

    **The critical caveat:** this must be in place **before the first personal data is written**.
    Retrofitting means re-encrypting an append-only history.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can a legal hold stop an erasure?" icon="gavel">
    Yes. A hold in force **refuses** the key destruction, and the refusal is audited with its reason.

    A hold also overrides artifact retention dates and audit partition drops — the hold wins.

    **One defect is recorded rather than hidden:** the interlock currently looks up holds by *subject*,
    and a hold scoped by *correlation id* — the natural scope for a case-level hold — names no
    subject, so it would not be found. That is a gap to close.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can we prove a report was actually filed?" icon="receipt">
    Yes, by the **stored receipt**. A run is not done until one comes back.

    The receipt itself is stored, not a pointer to the invocation that produced it — because the
    invocation is retained for months and the filing for years. A proof that expires on a schedule
    nobody intended is not a proof.

    Alongside it: the definition version that produced the file, the period, the watermark, the row
    count, and a **checksum** that makes reproducibility checkable rather than asserted.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Could a filing be submitted twice?" icon="clone">
    Not by design, and not by relying on the far side.

    Sending an idempotency header is correct and costs nothing, but *relying* on it is an assumption
    dressed as a contract — a third-party regulator gateway may honour it, ignore it, or implement it
    differently, and you find out during an incident.

    So at-most-once is achieved **on our side**: mark before calling, in its own transaction, then
    reconcile by querying status rather than blindly resending.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can we say &quot;nothing happened&quot; means nothing needed to happen?" icon="volume-xmark">
    Almost — and the distinction is exactly the one this design spends the most effort on.

    An event that produced nothing carries **which kind of nothing**:

    - `observed` — somebody **decided** it is worth keeping and worth nothing else. A decision, with
      an actor and a date.
    - `unmapped` — **nobody has looked at it yet**. A control gap, and a queue item.

    Because those are separate statuses, the `unmapped` queue can reach zero — which is what makes
    "the queue is empty" a meaningful statement.

    **The exception:** the platform cannot see occurrences that never became facts in the source
    system. 32 of those are itemised.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Who can read the audit trail, and is reading it recorded?" icon="eye">
    Reading is its own permission, not implied by access to the work the entries describe. **Exporting
    is a separate permission again**, because an export leaves the system.

    And yes — an export produces an audit entry with the exporter's name and the scope they chose.
    The columns are part of the export form on purpose, so that widening an export cannot happen
    quietly.

    **Open, recorded rather than filled in:** whether an export should require a stated *reason*. That
    is a compliance decision and it has not been taken.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="How do we know the record has not been tampered with?" icon="fingerprint">
    Hourly cryptographic seals over closed intervals. Verification recomputes and compares, and anyone
    with the permission can run it.

    **The honest limits:** a passing check proves the sealed entries are unchanged. It does **not**
    prove that every change produced an entry, and it does not cover the **unsealed tail** since the
    last seal.

    A change made and reverted **inside one unsealed interval** leaves the same evidence as no change.
    That detection window is the cost of periodic sealing, and periodic sealing was chosen because a
    per-row chain serialises every concurrent write for a property the seal already provides.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Someone with configuration access could change who gets notified. Is that controlled?" icon="user-lock">
    It is **detected and attributable**, not prevented.

    Configuration lives in a database rather than in git — a deliberate trade for "a new reaction must
    not require a deploy". The compensating controls are mandatory before production: a diffable
    configuration export, and the audit trail with a before/after diff and an actor on every change.

    The framing to take into an assessment: **an operator who can edit a workflow can change who is
    notified about a compliance failure. That is exactly as sensitive as a code deploy.** How that is
    supervised is a control-environment decision, and this documentation states the risk rather than
    minimising it.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Are statutory deadlines calculated correctly?" icon="calendar">
    **Not yet, and this is called out as an open question rather than assumed.**

    Timers are relative durations anchored at step entry. A statutory window is anchored to an
    external date and counted in **business days in the regulator's jurisdiction**. Nothing in the
    architecture owns a business-day calendar.

    A filing deadline computed in calendar days when the statute says business days is wrong in
    exactly the direction that matters. Both the calendar and the absolute-deadline anchor must be
    resolved before a statutory clock is relied on.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What happens to identity documents returned by a screening vendor?" icon="id-card">
    They are stored in the vendor-invocation log, which is deliberately retained for a **short** period
    — shorter than the audit record.

    **The field-level redaction policy and its retention are an open question that must be agreed with
    compliance before that module ships, not after.** That is stated as a hard sequencing requirement.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="When is any of this available?" icon="road">
    Nothing is running today. This is a design.

    Compliance sees the change in stages: **slice 2** makes human work attributable, **slice 3** brings
    the four-eyes control and vendor invocations, and **slice 4** brings reproducible reports with
    proof of filing.

    The one thing that cannot wait for its slice is crypto-shredding — it must exist before the first
    personal data is written.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
