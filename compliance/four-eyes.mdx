## The control

Some decisions require that the person who approves is **not** the person who acted. Two approvals in
sequence, by two different people, in two different queues.

This is the smallest possible high-stakes control, and the one most likely to be quietly wrong.

<Warning>
  Most controls fail by producing **no work** or **the wrong work**. This one fails by producing
  **work that looks complete** — an approval chain where one person played two roles.

  That is why it is enforced by the engine rather than by policy, training, or a checkbox.
</Warning>

## How it is enforced

The control does not say *"this needs four eyes"*. It **names a prior step**, and the engine resolves
that step's actor into a list of people to exclude from the current one.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The definition names the step to exclude">
    Not a person and not a boolean. In a chain of approvals, a boolean cannot say *which* prior step's
    actor should be excluded — it is not inferrable at all.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The reference is validated when the definition is published">
    Not when the case runs.

    A control referencing a step that some branch **skips** would fire or not fire depending on which
    way the case went — the worst behaviour available for a control, because it looks applied and is
    conditional.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The excluded person is never offered the task">
    Assignment resolves around them.
  </Step>
  <Step title="An attempt anyway is recorded as rejected">
    Not silently ignored. A denied attempt produces an audit entry with outcome `rejected`, committed
    in its own transaction precisely so the refusal survives the rollback of the refused action.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Excluding the maker — found as a gap, closed on 2026-07-28

The mechanism above excludes the actor of a **named step**. That left one person it could not reach.

<Warning>
  **The person who created the thing being approved acted *before the case existed*.** They are not a
  step actor — their identity arrives as the triggering event's actor.

  So the **first** approval — the one that matters most, because it is the first human to look at an
  address money will be sent to — could be assigned to the person who added it.

  This was not hypothetical. It is the one control the audited source system **did** implement, at
  both approval routes, as *creator ≠ approver*. A design that replaces that system must not lose it.
</Warning>

**The resolution: an explicit `excludeTriggerActor` flag.** The triggering event's actor is added to
**the same exclusion set** the step codes populate — one set, one mechanism, and the same failure
mode: if the exclusion empties the queue, it escalates, **never a silent self-approval**.

| Property | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Defaults to off** | So no already-published definition changes meaning until someone edits it |
| **A boolean is admissible here** | There is exactly **one** trigger actor per case, so it says nothing ambiguous — which is precisely why a bare "needs four eyes" boolean *is* rejected elsewhere |
| **Two alternatives recorded, not chosen** | Naming the trigger inside the exclusion list, or mapping the actor into the case's seed context and referencing it by path. Both are recorded with their costs |

<Note>
  **This was the only open item that was a control that was *off*, rather than a number or a name that
  was missing.** The others cost correctness later; this one was live. That is why it was closed
  first.
</Note>

### The other end of the same control

A related gap was closed the day before: **nothing marked a task type as an approval**, so *"the
control stopped because nobody is eligible"* was indistinguishable from *"the queue is merely quiet"*.

<Warning>
  An approval whose four-eyes exclusion leaves **nobody eligible** is a compliance gap. An ordinary
  unassigned task is a delayed one. They share a backend condition and **must not share a rendering.**
</Warning>

The marker lives on the task type, seeded per deployment. It could not be derived from the process
step, because `task` must stay usable for work that no process created.

## Why six copies of one rule was the argument for this layer

The audit of the existing estate found **six independent copies of one maker-checker rule**, in six
places, written six times.

<Note>
  That is the argument for a `task` layer expressed as a single number. Six implementations of one
  control means six places it can drift, six places it must be re-verified, and no single place a
  reviewer can look to confirm it is applied consistently.
</Note>
