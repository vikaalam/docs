Someone adds a bank account or a crypto address that money will later be sent to. Before it can
receive anything, **two other people must approve it, in order, and neither may be the person who
added it.**

This is the smallest possible high-stakes control, and the one most likely to be quietly wrong.

<Warning>
  Every other scenario in this set fails by producing **no work** or **the wrong work**. This one
  fails by producing **work that looks complete** — an approval chain where one person played two
  roles.
</Warning>

## Today, without the platform

Five distinct occurrences — a crypto address registered, a crypto address approved, a crypto address
management-approved, a bank account approved, a bank account management-approved — implemented as
**five separate code paths**.

Four of them **posted to one and the same outbound URL**, with:

- **no event type field**
- **no resource id**
- a four-key body whose only discriminator was which keys appeared inside a JSON string **nested
  inside the JSON body** — so the receiver had to parse twice and then sniff key names to learn
  whether it had been told about a bank account or a crypto address

<Note>
  This is the single sharpest structural finding in the whole audit, and it is why the event contract
  requires a type field on every event. **A destination should never have to guess what it just
  received.**
</Note>

## With the platform

<Steps>
  <Step title="Registration states a fact">
    `customer.payout_destination.registered` — carrying the instrument kind, the destination
    reference as the subject, and **the person who registered it as the actor**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="A case opens">
    One process definition covers both instrument kinds. A bank account and a crypto address are
    different objects with different fields and **identical governance**, so they share one control.
  </Step>
  <Step title="First approval — a human step in one queue">
    The reviewer sees the destination, the instrument kind, the fields relevant to that kind, and who
    registered it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Second approval — a human step in a different queue">
    A different role. The engine **excludes the first approver by name** — not by asking, and not by
    convention.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The destination becomes usable — or is rejected">
    Either way, a fact is published and recorded. A rejection carries the rejecting reviewer and the
    reason, both of which the starting condition dropped.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## How exclusion actually works

The control is not a boolean saying "needs four eyes". It **names a prior step**, and the engine
resolves that step's actor into a list of people to exclude.

<Note>
  **Why naming a step rather than a person is the correct design:** the engine needs a person id to
  exclude, and no boolean says *which* prior step's actor that is. In a chain of approvals it is not
  inferrable at all.

  There is also a publish-time validation: a control that references a step which some branch skips
  would fire or not fire depending on which way the case went — **the worst behaviour available for a
  control.** So the reference is validated when the definition is published, not when the case runs.
</Note>

## The finding this scenario surfaced — and how it was closed

This walkthrough found a real hole. It is worth reading as a worked example of the process, not just
the outcome.

<Warning>
  **The exclusion mechanism could not exclude the *maker*.**

  It accepts **step codes**, validated to be human steps within the definition. But the person who
  registered the destination **acted before the case existed** — they are not a step actor. Their
  identity arrives as the triggering event's `actor`.

  So the *first* approval — the one that matters most, because it is the first human to look at an
  address money will be sent to — **could be assigned to the person who added it.**

  Not hypothetical: it is the one control the audited source system **did** implement, at both
  approval routes, as *creator ≠ approver*. A design that replaces that system must not lose it.
</Warning>

**The walkthrough recorded three ways out with their costs and deliberately chose none.** That is the
convention: recording the gap is the instruction; inventing the mechanism is the expensive mistake.

| Option | Cost |
| --- | --- |
| **An explicit "exclude the triggering actor" flag** ← chosen | One boolean — the kind the design argues against elsewhere, **except** that there is exactly one trigger actor, so it says nothing ambiguous |
| Name the trigger inside the exclusion list | Keeps one field and one mental model; costs a magic value in a list that is otherwise step codes |
| Map the trigger actor into the case's seed context, referenced by path | Most general, largest surface, and re-opens the "definitions that can compute" question |

<Note>
  **Closed on 2026-07-28.** The trigger actor is added to **the same exclusion set** the step codes
  populate — one set, one mechanism, and the same failure mode: if the exclusion empties the queue it
  escalates, **never a silent self-approval**.

  It **defaults to off**, so no already-published definition changes meaning until someone edits it.

  This was the only open item that was a **control that was off**, rather than a number or a name that
  was missing — which is why it was closed first.
</Note>

Roughly thirty findings have arrived through this convention, three of them structural.

## What the design does prove here

| Proven | How |
| --- | --- |
| Naming a step rather than a person is correct | The engine needs a person id, and no boolean supplies one |
| Publish-time validation earns its cost | A control referencing a skippable step is worse than no control |
| Two instrument kinds, one control, three event types | Five source code paths collapse without losing any distinction anyone uses |
| The mandatory channel belongs on the **failure**, not on the approval | An approval sitting in a queue is visible; a failed one is not |
| **Nothing here required a deploy** | Three event types, two workflows, one process definition, two task types, two responsibility rules, four channel policies — all configuration |

- **Who registered it** — on the task, because the reviewer needs it and because the exclusion
  depends on it.
- **The instrument-specific fields** — a bank account and a crypto address ask different questions.
- **The stage you are at** — first or second approval, and who did the other one.
- **Your outcome and your reason**, both recorded.

<Warning>
  If you were the first approver, **you will not be offered the second approval**. An attempt to take
  it anyway is recorded as a **rejected action** — a denied attempt is itself part of the permanent
  record, not a silently ignored click.
</Warning>
